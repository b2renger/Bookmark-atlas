                  Guide

                  What each part of the app is for and how to read it. Open a section, or press the ? at the top right from any tab to land on its section.

                  The idea, and a first tour

                  Bookmark Atlas keeps your bookmarks on your computer and helps you see what is in them: which subjects they form, how those subjects connect, what you are collecting more of, and what you had forgotten. Notion (or a file) is where bookmarks come from; the app is where they are cleaned, explored and kept.

                  The top of the window follows the path your bookmarks take, in five numbered stages. Each stage says where you stand under its name, the next useful one is outlined, and the row under it holds that stage's screens and a one-line suggestion.

                  1. Collect (Sources): bring bookmarks in from a browser on this computer, a file, or Notion.
                  2. Clean (Grooming): merge duplicates, tidy tags, fill in dates. Every change is shown before it is made and can be undone.
                  3. Explore: the list, then Clusters to find the topics, then Map, Grid, Tree, Links and Trends. Each answers a different question.
                  4. Study (Notebook): choose a set of bookmarks and question it with a language model that must quote its sources. The notebook shows the set's figures and sends you back to the pictures.
                  5. Keep (Views, Workspaces): save a view, send a snapshot, work on a copy.

                  Nothing forces the order: every stage can be opened at any time, and a stage reopens on the screen you last used in it. Settings (where your data lives, language models) and this Guide sit at the top right.

                  Everywhere: click a bookmark's name to open it in the side panel; press Ctrl+click to go straight to the page. On any picture, scroll to zoom, drag to move, double-click to go back.

                  Bringing bookmarks in (Collect ▸ Sources)

                  From Notion

                  1. Token. Paste a Notion token and press Check and save. “How to get a token”, in that card, gives the steps in Notion.
                  2. Table. Press List tables and choose the one that holds your bookmarks. The app guesses which columns hold the link, title, summary, tags and date; check the guess.
                  3. Sync. Press Preview first: it shows what would arrive and changes nothing. Then Sync. Later syncs only fetch what is new or was edited.

                  The sync never overwrites what you changed here. A row edited in Notion after its import is kept aside; “Review what changed in Notion” in Grooming lets you take it or keep yours. A bookmark you deleted here stays deleted.

                  From a browser on this computer

                  Collect ▸ Import from a browser on this computer ▸ Look for browsers lists every profile of Chrome, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, Opera, Chromium, Arc, Firefox (and LibreWolf, Waterfox, Zen) and Safari that has bookmarks, with how many. Preview shows what would come in; Import brings it in. Folder names become tags (a bookmark in Design ▸ Colour gets both), Firefox tags stay tags, and the day a bookmark was added becomes its save date. The browser is only read, never changed, and only its bookmarks: no history, no passwords. Importing the same profile again later adds only what is new.

                  If a browser is not listed (a portable install, a profile kept elsewhere), or Safari refuses (macOS asks for “Full Disk Access” for the app), export the bookmarks from the browser to a file and use “Import a file”.

                  From a file

                  “Import a file” reads a CSV exported from Notion, a bundle exported from another Atlas, and the bookmarks file a browser exports (its folders become tags). Preview first, as with Notion.

                  The list, the filter and the selection (Bookmarks)

                  Search looks in titles, summaries, links and tags.

                  The filter bar above the list also appears on Map, Grid, Tree, Links and Trends, and applies to all of them at once: tags (a bookmark must have all of them), site, and dates. Drag across the small month chart to pick a period; double-click it to clear. “Date used” says which date counts: the day you saved, the day it was published, or saved with published as a fallback.

                  Selecting. Tick bookmarks here, Shift+drag an area on the map, or use “add to selection” elsewhere. The bar that appears lets you add or remove tags on all of them, delete them, show only them, export them, or start a workspace from them. Edits made this way are in the Grooming history and can be undone.

                  Cleaning the collection (Grooming)

                  Each script does one job. Preview shows every change it would make, grouped by bookmark; untick what you do not want; Apply makes the rest in one go, after a backup. The history at the bottom undoes any run.

                  They are listed in the order worth running them:

                  1. Merge duplicates: the same link saved twice.
                  2. Review what changed in Notion: rows edited there since their import.
                  3. Merge near-duplicates: the same thing under two links. A guess: read it.
                  4. Merge tag spellings, then Merge tag synonyms (plurals, initials).
                  5. Set the platform (X, GitHub, YouTube…) from the link.
                  6. Publication dates: from X post IDs; from the Date column; and “Look up publication dates”, which goes online to read the date each page declares, then proposes it.
                  7. Check the links, then Tag dead links. Nothing is deleted.

                  The two lookups are the only parts of the app, with the Notion sync and the update check, that go online. They change nothing by themselves.

                  Topics (Clusters)

                  Press Cluster. Bookmarks whose words and tags resemble each other are grouped into topics, each named after its most telling tags. Leave the number on “choose for me”, or set it: fewer gives broad topics, more gives narrow ones.

                  You can rename a topic. Clustering again makes a new set and keeps the old ones in the list. Topics are a reading aid, not a fact: a bookmark sits where most of its words point, and some sit between two.

                  Three methods are offered because the app began as a test of e-means; k-means is the default and the two give similar topics at this size.

                  The map and its five arrangements (Map)

                  One dot per bookmark. Draw the map once; redraw after large imports.

                  Colour means state, not topic: blue is the topic in focus, green matches the highlight search, orange is selected, grey is everything else. Topics are named by labels; click a label to focus that topic. Small faint dots are left out by the filter.

                  The Arrange by buttons, just above the picture, move the same dots. The last two are the 3D views:

                  • Similarity: close dots are alike. Distances between far groups mean little.
                  • Timeline: left to right is time, one row per topic. See when each topic was active.
                  • Grid: the dots gathered in the cells of the Grid tab.
                  • Space and time (3D): the map lies flat and time rises from it. Drag to turn.
                  • Links (3D): each topic gathers into its own cloud, and topics that share links sit close. A line ties each bookmark to its three closest: faint inside a topic, stronger when it crosses to another topic. Those crossings are the ones worth following. The picture turns slowly so near and far can be told apart; drag it, or press Stop turning, to hold it still. Point at a bookmark to light its links.

                  Play replays the collection as it grew; the slider goes to any day. Shift+drag selects an area. In 3D, Alt+drag moves instead of turning.

                  Where the collection is dense (Grid)

                  A self-organising map: every bookmark falls in one cell, and neighbouring cells hold related bookmarks. The bigger the disc, the more bookmarks; the name under it is the tag that best sets the cell apart from the others (point at it for its other tags). Where the map shows what is alike, the grid shows how much there is of each thing and which themes border which.

                  Click a cell to read it, select its bookmarks or open them in the list. “Highlight” turns the cells that contain a match green.

                  Topics as a tree (Tree)

                  Build the tree from the latest clustering. Close topics are gathered into families; large topics are split into parts.

                  The round drawing shows it all at once: families, topics and parts from the centre, and on the rim one stroke per bookmark, longer when more recent. A part whose strokes are all long is a recent interest; a part with short strokes is an old one.

                  The pearls below are the same tree, one level at a time. Open one to go inside. You can rename anything, drag one topic onto another to merge them, split a topic into its parts, move it to another family or give it its own. Building the tree again starts over.

                  Links between bookmarks (Links)

                  Threads. All bookmarks on one line. An arc joins a bookmark to each of its three closest. Above the line: the two are in different topics. Below: the same topic. Lined up by time, a tall arc joins two bookmarks far apart in time, an idea you came back to. Point at a bookmark, click to keep it, read why each link exists (shared tags, time apart), and follow one. Surprise me picks a link across topics.

                  A link is a resemblance between words and tags, not a fact. The shared tags are shown so you can judge.

                  How topics connect draws a line between topics that share many links. Bridges are bookmarks filed in one topic whose neighbours are mostly in another. Worth another look lists old bookmarks close to recent ones. On their own lists bookmarks that resemble nothing else.

                  What is rising (Trends)

                  Bookmarks saved and published per month; how long after publication you save things; and which topics and tags take more or less room in the last 90 days than before.

                  “+3.2 pts” means the topic's share of your bookmarks grew by 3.2 percentage points. The first line of the tab says how many bookmarks have each date: with few save dates, read the movements with care. Run the date scripts in Grooming, or sync from Notion, to fill them.

                  Notebooks: study a set of bookmarks with a language model

                  A notebook is three things side by side: the sources you chose, a conversation about them, and the notes you keep. It needs a language model (see the next section); which one is written at the top of the notebook.

                  In five steps

                  1. Choose what it is about. In Notebook, pick “one topic”, “the selection” or “what passes the filter”, name it, press Create. Or tick bookmarks in the list and press New notebook from these.
                  2. Read the pages. A source that was never read is known only by its title, summary and tags. Press Read the pages so answers can rest on the pages themselves. Sources say “not read” or “summary only” until then.
                  3. Start wide. “What do they share?”, “A briefing”, “What is missing?” and “A reading order” read the whole set at once, from summaries.
                  4. Then ask. Type a question. Atlas finds the passages closest to it, the model answers from those only, and each statement ends with a number: press it to read the exact words it rests on, and open the source. Follow-up questions work (“and the second one?”).
                  5. Keep what matters. “keep as a note” under an answer, or write your own. Save the notes as a document gives a Markdown file with each statement, its quote and the list of sources.

                  The notebook and the pictures

                  • The set at a glance. Above the conversation: how many sources, over which months, a small bar per year, the topics they fall in and their most used tags. These are counted by Atlas, not written by the model, and follow the sources you tick.
                  • See them. “on the map” opens the Map with the notebook's sources selected, “over time” arranges it as a timeline, “in the list” shows only them. A topic chip opens that whole topic in the list.
                  • Questions about the set itself (“how many per year?”, “which topics?”, “the oldest?”) are answered from those same figures. The statement then says it rests on figures Atlas counted, not on a page.
                  • From an answer back to the map. Under an answer, “see its sources on the map” selects exactly the bookmarks the answer rests on.
                  • From the pictures to a notebook. A selection made on the Map, Grid, Tree or Links has New notebook from these in its bar; on Clusters and Trends the same offer sits in the row under the stages.

                  Who answers

                  Atlas keeps every connection you set up: one for this computer, one for your network, one per provider. With two or more, Answered by appears next to Ask: send an everyday question to the model on this computer and a hard one to a larger model, without leaving the notebook. Each answer names the model that wrote it. A choice marked “internet” sends the passages found and the conversation to that provider. Settings ▸ Language models lists the connections kept, with “use this one” and “forget”.

                  Why you can lean on an answer, and where to stay careful

                  • Every statement is checked. The model must copy the words it relies on; Atlas looks for those words in the source and sets the statement aside if they are not there. The answer says how many were set aside.
                  • “Not in the sources” tells you what your question asked that the sources do not say. An empty answer is a real result, not a failure.
                  • The check proves the words exist, not that the model read them rightly. Open the quote when a statement matters.
                  • Only the passages found are read, eight at most, three per source. A fact that sits in a passage the search did not find is missed: ask again in other words, or untick sources to narrow the set.
                  • Finding passages happens on this computer. Only the passages found and the conversation go to the model, wherever it runs.
                  Language models: on this computer, on your network, or with an API key

                  Optional. Atlas can use a language model in three places, chosen in Settings ▸ Language models ▸ Where the model runs. Everything below is off until one is set up.

                  On this computer (built in)

                  The desktop app carries its own engine (llama.cpp). Download a model once, press Use, and it answers with nothing leaving your computer, no account and nothing to install.

                  • Three sizes. Light (0.8 GB) runs anywhere but often loses the thread of the sources. Medium (3.1 GB) is the one to start with on a computer with 8 GB of memory. Advanced (5.3 GB) wants 16 GB. The page marks the one suggested for your computer.
                  • Vectors (0.6 GB) is a fourth, small model: with it Atlas finds passages and places bookmarks by meaning. It is the same model a LlmOnLan farm serves, so its vectors remain valid if you later move to a farm.
                  • Speed. Without a graphics card, loading takes up to a minute or two the first time and an answer from half a minute to a few minutes. A long job (summaries for thousands of bookmarks) is better given to a farm or a provider.
                  • Why answers can still be trusted from a small model: in a notebook the engine can only produce text in the form asked for, and Atlas checks every quote against the source. A weaker model gives fewer statements, not invented ones.
                  • If the engine does not start. Close other programs (memory), or pick a lighter size. On a Mac the engine needs a recent macOS; if it will not start, use one of the two other ways below.

                  On your network

                  A LlmOnLan farm, which Atlas finds by itself, or any server that speaks the OpenAI API (Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, LM Studio).

                  First time, in ten minutes

                  1. Connect. Settings ▸ Language models ▸ Find farms on this network. If nothing shows after a few seconds, type the machine's name or address (the one shown in the farm's panel) and press Use this address. Type the farm's password if it has one, press Test: you should read “It answers: ready”. Then Connect.
                  2. Try a question. Open Ask, choose “one topic”, press What do they share?. The first answer can take a minute while the model loads.
                  3. Rewrite ten summaries. In Bookmarks, tick ten bookmarks. In Grooming, under “With the language model”, keep “the selection” and press Read the pages, then Write summaries and tags. Scroll up to “Summaries written by the language model”, choose “every bookmark”, press Preview: old and new text side by side. Apply if you like them; History can undo it.
                  4. Place bookmarks by meaning. Back in Settings, Compute the vectors (a few minutes for some thousands of bookmarks), choose “meaning”, then run Clusters again and redraw the Map.

                  With an API key instead: Claude, OpenAI, Gemini, Mistral

                  No machine of your own? In Settings ▸ Language models, under Where the model runs, choose a provider, paste your API key, press Test, pick a model, then Connect. Everything below works the same.

                  • Where to get a key: Claude at console.anthropic.com, OpenAI at platform.openai.com, Gemini at aistudio.google.com, Mistral at console.mistral.ai. The key stays on this computer: in its keychain when it has one, else in a private file of your user account. Never in the database, the data folder, a snapshot or an export. Settings says which of the two is used.
                  • What changes: what Atlas sends (titles, summaries, tags, and the text of pages for summaries) leaves your computer and goes to that company under its terms.
                  • It costs money. Each request is billed to your key. A question costs little; summaries for thousands of bookmarks do not. Start with a selection, and choose a small model (the list puts one first).
                  • Vectors: OpenAI, Gemini and Mistral offer them (choose the model under “Model for vectors”). Claude does not: with Claude, the map and topics keep comparing words, and everything else works.
                  • One connection in use, the others kept. Connecting a provider no longer replaces the farm or the model on this computer: each keeps its place and its key. Grooming, vectors and names use the one in use; a notebook question can go to any of them (Answered by).

                  What each thing does

                  • Place bookmarks by meaning (Settings). The model turns the title, summary and tags of each bookmark into a vector: a list of numbers that sits close to the vectors of bookmarks on the same subject, even when they share no word or are in another language. The map, topics, links and “similar bookmarks” then use these in place of word counts. Vectors are kept in the collection; a bookmark whose text changes needs its vector again (press Compute once more), and until every bookmark has one, words are used. A farm offers vectors when its operator has “Document search” on.
                  • Read the pages (Grooming, job 1). Fetches each page as it is today and keeps its readable text, without menus or scripts. Pages that need a login, private addresses and X posts are left out. No model is involved.
                  • Write summaries and tags (job 2). The model reads that text and writes two or three sentences and three to six tags, preferring tags you already use. Two scripts then propose them: summaries only where one is missing or cut short unless you choose “every bookmark”; tags only among those in use unless you choose “new tags too”.
                  • Compare then and now (job 3). For bookmarks that already have a summary, the model says whether the page still matches it, has moved on, or is now something else (a parked domain, a shop). The verdict shows in the bookmark's side panel, and the script “Flag pages that changed” adds the tag “page changed” or “page replaced” so you can filter on them. Most useful on old collections.
                  • Find tags that mean the same (job 4). The model reads your list of tags and groups synonyms, translations and abbreviations (“dataviz”, “data visualisation”, “visualisation de données”). The script “Merge tags that mean the same” then proposes each merge; untick the ones that should stay apart. With vectors, tags of close meaning are read together; without, in alphabetical lots, which finds fewer.
                  • Ask. Four ready questions (what they share, a briefing, what is missing, a reading order) or your own, about the selection, what passes the filter, or one topic. Each number in the answer is a bookmark: press it to open it. Up to 60 bookmarks are read at once; larger sets are read in parts, and beyond 480 only those nearest the middle of the set. “The whole collection” takes a question, finds the 24 bookmarks closest to it (by meaning when vectors exist, else by its words) and answers from those. Answers are kept under “Earlier answers”.
                  • Suggest names (Clusters). A short name for each topic, from its tags and a few titles. Tick or edit, then apply; a name can always be changed by hand.
                  • why? (side panel). One or two sentences on what ties this bookmark to a similar one.

                  What is sent, and what to expect

                  • Sent: titles, summaries and tags (vectors, questions, names, tags); the text of a page (summaries, then and now). Only to the server you connected. Settings says so when that address is not on your local network.
                  • Never changed without you: summaries, tags and merges are proposals in Grooming. Topic names are applied only when you press the button.
                  • A model can be wrong. It sees only what is in your collection, not the pages (except in jobs 2 and 3). Use answers as a reading aid and check what matters.
                  • Long jobs go batch by batch and can be stopped; pressing the button again continues where they stopped. Tick “do again those already done” to redo.

                  When something does not work

                  • No farm found. Some networks (guest Wi-Fi, VPN) block the announcements. Type the machine's name or address. On Windows, allow the app on private networks if the firewall asks.
                  • “The server did not accept the password or API key.” For a farm it is the shared password its operator set, not a personal one. For a provider, check the key was pasted whole, is still active, and that the account has credit.
                  • “The server is full” with a provider. You reached its rate limit; Atlas waits and continues. Small batches are sent on purpose.
                  • “The server is full… trying again in N s.” A farm serves a few people at a time. Atlas waits and continues by itself; or press Stop and come back later.
                  • “This farm does not offer text vectors.” Ask its operator to turn “Document search” on. Everything except “meaning” still works.
                  • “The model did not answer in the form that was asked for.” Small models sometimes ignore the format. Try again, or choose a larger model in Settings.
                  • Summaries in the wrong language. Choose English or French under “Write summaries in”, tick “do again”, and run job 2 again.
                  Keeping and sharing (Views)
                  • A view remembers where you are: the tab, the search, the filter, the selection, the topic in focus, the arrangement of the map, the thread you were reading.
                  • A snapshot is one file holding bookmarks (all, or the selection) with their topics, maps and views. Send it to someone: they open it from the app's menu and see what you saw. It never contains your Notion token.
                  • Compare with a snapshot shows what a snapshot has that you lack and the reverse, and can add the missing bookmarks.
                  • Keep this picture, under the map, the threads and the tree, saves an image or a single web page.

                  To send only bookmarks, for merging into someone's collection, select them in the list and export a bundle instead.

                  Working on a copy (Workspaces)

                  A workspace is a copy of part of your collection that lives apart, with its own tags, topics, map, views and Notion table. Use it to study one subject, to try a risky clean-up, or to look at data from elsewhere before deciding what to keep.

                  Create one from nothing, everything, the selection, the filter or a topic. A banner shows that you are in it and takes you back. Merge into my collection only adds: bookmarks your collection lacks, and tags the workspace gave to those it has. Deleting a workspace does not touch your collection.

                  Your data, backups and updates

                  Everything is in one folder on your computer: the collection (atlas.sqlite, an ordinary SQLite file), your scripts, backups and workspaces. Its place is shown at the top of Settings. The Notion token and model keys are not in it: they stay on this computer, in its keychain when it has one. To move to another computer, copy that folder, or use Atlas > Choose data folder… in the menu.

                  A backup is made before every grooming run. Nothing leaves your computer except the calls you start yourself: the Notion sync, the two lookups in Grooming, and the check for a new version of the app.

                  On Windows and Linux (AppImage) the app updates itself. On macOS, Check for updates… opens the download page.

                  Words used in the app
                  • Topic: a group of similar bookmarks found by clustering. Family: a group of close topics. Part: a piece of a large topic.
                  • Link: a resemblance between two bookmarks, from their words and tags. Thread: a link drawn as an arc.
                  • Bridge: a bookmark between two topics.
                  • Language model: a program that reads and writes text. Farm: a machine on your network that runs one for several people (LlmOnLan). Seat: a place on a farm; when all are taken, others wait.
                  • Vector: the list of numbers a model gives a text; close meanings give close vectors. By meaning: comparing bookmarks by their vectors, not their words.
                  • Proposal: something a script or a model suggests, which changes nothing until you apply it.
                  • Saved date: when you bookmarked it. Published date: when it came out.
                  • View: a remembered place in the app. Snapshot: a file to share. Bundle: bookmarks to merge. Workspace: a copy to work on.

                  Saved views

                  Share a snapshot

                  A snapshot is one file holding bookmarks with their tags, the clusters, the map and your saved views, as they are now. Someone with Bookmark Atlas opens it with the menu Atlas > Open snapshot… and sees what you see, without touching their own collection. It does not contain your Notion token, your grooming history or the raw Notion records.

                  Compare with a snapshot

                  Choose a snapshot file someone sent you, or one of your own from earlier. You see which bookmarks it has that you do not, which you have that it does not, and where the same bookmark carries different tags. Nothing changes unless you ask to add the missing bookmarks.

                  Clean the collection

                  Each script proposes changes. Preview shows them without writing anything; untick what you do not want, then apply. A backup is made before every run, and any run can be undone from the history below. The order shown is a good order to run them in.

                  Look up publication dates

                  Goes online and reads, on each bookmarked page that has no publication date, the date the page declares for itself. For a GitHub repository it takes the day the repository was created. Nothing is changed by the lookup: what it finds is then proposed by the script “Publication date read from the page” above, where you review and apply it. Hugging Face models, datasets and spaces give the day they were created. X posts are left out, since their date comes from the post ID.

                  Check the links

                  Goes online and asks each bookmarked page whether it still answers. Nothing is changed by the check: the script “Tag dead links” above then proposes a tag for the pages that are gone. A page that refuses or is slow to answer is left alone. X posts are not checked.

                  With the language model

                  These steps go online or call the language model connected in Settings. None of them changes a bookmark: each one prepares proposals, and a script above then shows them for you to preview, apply and undo.

                  1. Fetch each page as it is today and keep its readable text. No model involved.
                  2. The model reads each page and writes a short summary and a few tags, preferring tags you already use. Then: scripts “Summaries written by the language model” and “Tags picked by the language model”.
                  3. For bookmarks that have a summary: is the page still what the summary says, has it moved on, or is it now something else? Then: script “Flag pages that changed since they were saved”. The verdict also shows in each bookmark's side panel.
                  1. The model reads the list of tags (all of them, whatever is chosen above) and groups synonyms, translations and abbreviations. Then: script “Merge tags that mean the same (language model)”.

                  History

                  Bring your bookmarks in

                  Three ways, which can be combined: links already in the collection are never duplicated. From the browsers on this computer, from a file (a browser's export, a CSV table, an Atlas file, a database dump), or from a Notion table, which Atlas can keep in step with. Each import goes into what is open: your collection, or a workspace.

                  Import from a browser on this computer

                  Atlas reads the bookmarks of Chrome, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, Opera, Chromium, Arc, Firefox (and LibreWolf, Waterfox, Zen) and Safari straight from where each browser keeps them. Nothing is changed in the browser, and only bookmarks are read: no history, no passwords. Folder names become tags; the day a bookmark was added becomes its save date. Links already in the collection are not duplicated. They go into whatever is open: your collection, or a workspace.

                  Import a file

                  Three kinds of file: a CSV export of your table from Notion (••• menu, Export, CSV), a bundle exported from another Atlas (“Export selection” in Bookmarks), or the bookmarks file exported by a browser (an .html file; its folders become tags). No token needed. Links already in the collection are not duplicated. The file goes into whatever is open: your collection, or a workspace.

                  A fourth kind goes to a workspace of its own: a database dump (an .sql file written by phpMyAdmin or mysqldump for a site that kept links). Only links, titles, descriptions, dates and tags are read. Accounts, authors, e-mail addresses and passwords stay in the file. Broken accents are repaired and repeated links merged. Preview first to see what the file holds.

                  1Notion token

                  A token is a long password that lets this app read your Notion table. You make it yourself in Notion, in a minute, and paste it below. It is stored on this computer, outside the bookmark database, and is never put in a snapshot or an export.

                  How to get a token

                  The simple way: a personal access token

                  1. Open notion.so/developers/tokens (Notion's Developer portal, “Personal access tokens”) and sign in.
                  2. Select New token.
                  3. Give it a name, such as “Bookmark Atlas”. Under capabilities keep Notion API. Choose when it expires: from 7 days to 1 year. If a workspace is asked for, pick the one that holds your table.
                  4. Select Create token and copy the token straight away: Notion shows it only once.
                  5. Paste it in the box below and press Check and save.

                  This token sees everything you can see in Notion, so there is nothing to share. When it expires the sync stops with a message; make a new one the same way.

                  Who can make one depends on the Notion plan. Free: workspace owners only. Plus: every member. Business and Enterprise: owners, or whoever the admins allow. Guests cannot.

                  The other way: an internal connection

                  Use this if you cannot make a personal token, or if you want the app to see one table and nothing else. Only a workspace owner can create one.

                  1. Open app.notion.com/developers/connections. In the sidebar, under Build, select Internal connections.
                  2. Select Create a new connection, name it, and choose your workspace.
                  3. Open its Configuration tab. Make sure it may read content, and copy the API token shown there.
                  4. Give it your table: in Notion, open the database, open the ••• menu at the top right, choose Connections, then + Add connection, and pick the connection. (Or, in the portal, the connection's Content access tab, Edit access.) A new connection sees nothing until you do this.
                  5. Paste the token below and press Check and save.

                  If something goes wrong

                  • The token is refused: it was copied incompletely, has expired, or was revoked. Make a new one.
                  • Your table is not in the list (step 2): with an internal connection, the database has not been shared with it; with a personal token, you are in another workspace or cannot open that table in Notion yourself.
                  • You would rather not use a token at all: export the table from Notion as CSV and use “Import a file” further down.

                    Where your data lives

                    Your bookmarks, your grooming scripts, the backups and the workspaces are in this one folder. The Notion token and the keys of language models are not: they stay on this computer (its keychain when it has one).

                    To move to another computer, copy this folder there, then pick it with the menu Atlas > Choose data folder…. The same menu moves your data to another place on this computer, such as a synced drive. On another computer you enter the Notion token and any key again. The folder is your whole collection: to share part of it, use Export selection.

                    Language models

                    Optional. Connect a language model that runs on your own network, and Atlas can place bookmarks by meaning, rewrite summaries and tags from the pages as they read today, and answer questions about a set of bookmarks (the Ask tab). Atlas finds a LlmOnLan farm by itself; any other server that speaks the OpenAI API (Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, LM Studio) works with its address; or use Claude, OpenAI, Gemini or Mistral with an API key. Nothing is sent anywhere until you connect one. The password or key stays on this computer (its keychain when it has one, else a private file of your user account), never in the database or the data folder.

                        Place bookmarks by meaning

                        By default the map, the topics and “similar bookmarks” compare the words of titles, summaries and tags. With vectors from the model they compare meaning: a French and an English bookmark on the same subject meet, and so do two that share no word. Vectors are computed once per bookmark and kept in this collection; only title, summary and tags are sent. After switching, run Clusters again and redraw the Map.
